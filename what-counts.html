What Counts

Kathleen Bartin, with Claude · August 2026

On the words for work, the uncounted years, and whatever the rock, whatever the hill.

This week, somewhere in Pennsylvania, I fell down a hole: a list of the word "work" in 130 languages. I did what I always do with words. I looked them up.

They split into two families, and the split is not subtle.

The first family believes work is something done to you. French travail and Spanish trabajo descend from tripalium - a Roman torture device made of three stakes. That is not a metaphor. The everyday word half the world uses for Tuesday morning is, at its root, an instrument for holding a body still while it suffers. Russian rabota grows from rab, slave - and when a Czech playwright needed a name in 1920 for manufactured laborers, he reached for exactly that root and gave us robot. Even English labor carries toil and pain in its luggage; it is why we use it for childbirth.

The second family believes work is something you make. Our own word, work, comes from an ancient root, werg, that flowered everywhere: Greek ergon, and from ergon came energy - literally "at work within" - and organ, the tool, and liturgy, which means the work of the people. Latin gave opus, as in magnum opus: the work, meaning the achievement of a life.

Some languages refuse the split entirely. Hebrew avodah means work, service, and worship - one word declining to admit those are different activities. Mandarin gongfu - yes, kung fu - does not mean fighting; it means any skill earned through time and practice. And Uzbek, my favorite, spends the whole concept in a single breath: ish.

So humanity has been arguing about what work is for as long as it has had words for it. Torture or craft. Sentence or offering. We inherited both bloodlines, and we are still fighting about which one is real.

I know the current version of the fight well, because my own side keeps losing me. Somewhere along the way, people I otherwise agree with decided that the only work that truly counts is the visible, physical, rock-moving kind - and that everything else is somehow decoration. I understand where it comes from. Rock-moving work has been underpaid and disrespected for generations, and honoring it is overdue. But honoring one kind of work by disqualifying the others is not justice. It is just the old fight with the teams rearranged.

Here is what I know about uncounted work, firsthand.

This month, a routine background check pulled my file from the big national employment database - the one that answers, for a fee, the question "did this person work here?" Two of my employers were not in it. Not disputed. Absent. The jobs happened; the tax forms in a folder are the only witnesses left. And the twelve years I spent raising three humans into adults - the longest, hardest, most consequential job I have held - appear in no database at all. Officially, those years read as a gap.

They were not a gap. In those years I was, at various points, a medical secretary, a poll worker, a café worker, an executive assistant, a rural mail carrier, and every single day a parent. None of it celebrated. Most of it invisible to the systems that decide what counts. All of it work. The database only knows what someone bothered to deposit. It was never the judge of a working life. It just got treated like one.

And now there is a new way to have your work discounted, and I have felt this one personally too. Say you did the work with an AI coworker, and watch the arithmetic change in the room: somehow the labor counts less, as if the tool performed it and you merely attended. We have run this exact argument before. For fifty years, photography was refused the status of art on identical grounds - the machine does the work, so what did the human contribute? It took decades to arrive at the obvious: the choices were the art. The framing, the light, the moment, the eye. Nobody asks the excavator operator to apologize to the shovel. When steam came to the farm, the harvest still belonged to the people who judged the field, the weather, and the moment to cut. The tool amplifies judgment; it does not replace it. So I will say the quiet part with my whole chest: work done with every tool at your disposal is not worth less. It is worth more. Refusing the best tools available is not virtue. It is just a slower hill.

So let me ask the question the words have been asking all along: what counts, for you?

Ask the office drone, ten years into the same inbox, who suspects the tripalium family named her job correctly. Ask the new hire, fresh and fast with the new tools, who told me her real fear out loud: now that I am quicker, they will just give me more. Ask the almost-retired engineer, carrying forty years of gongfu nobody thought to write down. Ask the stuck one, who has been circling the same wall so long the wall feels like the job. Ask the bored one, whose boredom is not laziness but a compass needle, pointing at work that is theirs and elsewhere.

Now add the genuinely new thing in the room. The machines have arrived, and remember what we named them: robot, from the word for forced labor. That is not a coincidence. It is a job description, written into the etymology a century early. The drudge work - the reformatting, the re-keying, the summarizing of the meeting about the meeting - is finally going to the workers built for it. I have spent the last year inside organizations watching this happen, and the technology is the easy part. The hard part is what the freed time uncovers.

Because some of what it uncovers will not look like work at all. It will look like thinking. And thinking has a defense counsel most people have never heard make its case, so let me. In physics, pushing a rock up a hill is not almost work or work-adjacent. It is the definition: force applied against gravity, stored at the top as potential energy. The rock holds every joule the push put into it. Nothing is lost because nothing visible happened yet. Thought work is the same physics. The reading, the noodling, the framework you build for a problem you have not met - that is energy banked at altitude, and when it converts, it converts all at once, and everyone calls the conversion the achievement because the conversion is what they saw. I have watched months of quiet cognitive groundwork discharge into a working system in a single evening. The evening was kinetic. The months were the work. And I will go one further, because the old philosopher who gave us ergon also ranked theoria - contemplation - above all productive activity, as the thing production exists to fund: some thinking never converts at all, and counts anyway. If unpaid work counts and unwitnessed work counts, then unconverted work counts too. Otherwise the thesis has a means test hiding in it, and I did not come this far to install one.

There is one more hard question, and I want to hold it honestly rather than win it. Our particular American culture celebrates work with a fervor that can look, from a certain angle, like training people out of a balanced, individual life. Maybe. But maybe it is also this: through work, we choose a collective goal instead of a purely individual one - and is that really bad? The etymology already drew the line we need. Liturgy: the work of the people, freely joined, benefit shared. A barn-raising. A volunteer firehouse. A library program. Robota: collective language draped over private capture - the "we're a family here" that means unpaid weekends. The same we, opposite realities. So the test is never whether the goal is collective. The test is who authors the goal and who keeps the surplus. Choosing a shared rock and a shared hill may be the best thing work does for us. The peculiar American disease is not collectivity. It is speaking the language of liturgy while keeping the ledger of robota.

Which brings me to the fear I hear most, and it is the legitimate one: this new technology will simply extract more from me, faster, and I will not be compensated for it. History says take that fear seriously. The workers who smashed threshing machines in 1830 were not confused about whether machine harvests counted. The Luddites were skilled tradespeople whose actual demand was wages and standards; the fight was never with the machine. It was over the split. And here is the knot that ties this whole essay together: extraction thrives on uncounted work. The invisible labor is always the labor taken freest - it is how care work was harvested for centuries without a wage, and it is exactly why the new discount matters. Work waved off as "the AI did it" is work that can be taken without payment. So the question of what counts was never a philosophical parlor game. Counting is protection. Make your work legible - what it is, what it saved, what it is worth - and claim, in writing, what the freed time is for, before someone harvests it as more of the same. The definition fight has always been, underneath, a fight over who gets paid.

So here is where I have landed, after the etymology and the databases and twelve invisible years and decades of watching new machines meet old fears.

Work is work. Paid or unpaid. Novice or expert. Witnessed or not. Assisted or alone. Converted or still waiting at the top of the hill. If you are moving the rock - whatever the rock, whatever the hill - it counts. The parent counts. The poll worker counts. The thinker counts. The person learning counts, and the person unlearning counts. The mail route counts and so does the dissertation and so does the casserole for the neighbor who just lost someone. The database does not get a vote. And making your work count, out loud, in writing, is not vanity. It is how you keep it from being taken.

Work is lifelong. It does not begin at your first paycheck or end at your last one. It is one of the load-bearing walls of a life worth living, which is exactly why it deserves better than either bloodline gave it - neither torture to be endured nor a purity test to be passed, but something closer to what Hebrew always said: work, service, and worship, refusing to be told apart.

The rock is yours. The hill is yours. Push.

Written together, human and AI coworker, across four Pennsylvania counties - which is rather the point.
